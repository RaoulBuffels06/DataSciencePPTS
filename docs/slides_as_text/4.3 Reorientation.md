## Slide 5
- Scrub / Data Wrangling (once you got data….)

- Steps during scrub
- Inspect the dataset: shape, info(), head()
- Handle missing values: remove, fill, or impute
- Remove duplicates & inconsistencies: e.g., inconsistent spellings, wrong datatypes
- Detect & treat outliers: using statistical or visual methods (boxplot, z-score)
- Transform data:
- Normalize / standardize numeric columns.
- Encode categorical variables (one-hot, label encoding)
- Rename columns / fix datatypes for clarity

## Slide 8
- What does an EDA include?

- Descriptive statistics
- Central tendency → what is a “typical” value in the data? (e.g., average, median)
- Spread/variability → how much do the values differ from each other? (e.g., wide vs narrow distribution)
- Shape of distribution → is the data symmetric, skewed, or multi-peaked?
- Unusual values → spotting anomalies or outliers that might distort the analysis
- Univariate analysis (single variable)
- Numeric: histogram, boxplot, violin plot
- Categorical: bar plot, value counts
- Bivariate / multivariate analysis
- Numeric vs numeric: scatterplot, correlation heatmap
- Numeric vs categorical: boxplot, violin plot
- Multivariate: pair plot, clustering patterns
- Time series / trends
- Line plots, moving averages, seasonal trends

## Slide 12
- Findings vs. Conclusions

- Findings = What the data shows (facts)
- Factual results from the data/EDA
- e.g., “Sales are 20% higher in Region A than Region B.”
- Conclusions = What it might mean (interpretation)
- Interpretation + implication for the business
- e.g., “Region A is outperforming, so resources should be shifted there.”
- Rule of thumb:
- Findings = “what the data shows.”
- Conclusions = “what the decision-maker should take away.”

- 12

## Slide 13
- ⚠️ Important

- EDA gives findings for sure
- Conclusions are often hypotheses, not proofs ⚠️
- To confirm conclusions → need models, experiments, or external context
- EDA = safe for findings, cautious for conclusions

## Slide 14
- From Findings to Hypotheses

- What is a hypothesis?
- A testable statement about a possible relationship or pattern in the data.
- Goes beyond describing what you see → suggests why it might happen.

- 14

## Slide 15
- How to form a hypothesis

- Start with a finding
- e.g., “Region A has 20% higher sales than Region B.”
- Ask ‘Why?’
- Could it be marketing spend? Customer demographics? Seasonality?
- Propose a possible explanation (your hypothesis)
- “Higher sales in Region A are due to a stronger online marketing campaign.”
- Make it testable
- Identify data or methods that could confirm or reject it.

- 15

## Slide 16
- Good hypothesis checklist

- Clear: one specific idea, not vague
- Testable: can be checked with data or further analysis
- Relevant: linked to the business question
- EDA not as the end, but as the start of deeper inquiry.

- 16

## Slide 17
- Examples

- 1. Predictive hypothesis
- "Students who spend at least 3 hours per week on online exercises are more likely to score above 80% in the Data Science course."
- 2. Correlation hypothesis
- "There is a positive correlation between the number of hours a person exercises per week and their average sleep duration."
- 3. Group comparison hypothesis
- "Employees who participate in team projects score on average 10% higher on quarterly performance evaluations than those who work individually."
- 4. Intervention effect hypothesis
- "Sending weekly reminder emails increases the likelihood that users will remain active on a mobile app after one month."
- 5. Behavioral hypothesis
- "Customers who frequently open promotional emails are more likely to make a purchase within 30 days than those who do not."
